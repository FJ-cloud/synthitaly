Section A

Where the money comes in

A household's income is drawn once, at construction, and then paid on one day a month — the 27th (numbers.py:158, a stand-in for the last business day). There is no weekly or biweekly pay, no mid-month top-up, and no income shock during a run: employment status is fixed for the whole simulation.

Each source has its own mean and its own spread. The multipliers are mean-preserving — they are rescaled so the share-weighted population mean stays at the SHIW figure, which is what keeps the income quartile and quintile bands (and therefore all the debt and savings calibration) undisturbed (numbers.py:568-581, asserted at import).

No taxes, no VAT, no social-security contributions, and no household-to-household transfers exist anywhere in the model. SHIW incomes are already net of contributions, and the bank-eye framing puts tax out of scope. This is a scope decision, not an omission — but it means the model cannot say anything about fiscal policy, and the word 'income' here always means take-home.

The five recurring bills

Subscription is an independent draw per bill, so a household can hold rent and a mortgage at once, or neither. The survey gives only the marginal shares; the independence is an assumption.

Bill amounts carry no dispersion. Every utilities bill in the model is exactly €124, every month, for every household. Purchase tickets get a lognormal spread; bills get a point mass. Nothing in the survey requires this — it is a simplification, and it makes the monthly obligation far more predictable than a real one.

Two overlapping views of debt

The model carries both the mortgage / consumer_loan recurring bills (Payment Behaviour Survey) and a separate aggregate debt_service line (SHIW). They come from different surveys, measure overlapping things, and are deliberately not reconciled. A household can pay both. This is documented as a teaching-prototype simplification, but it does mean total debt burden is overstated for anyone holding both.

Section C

The order things happen in

Order matters here more than it looks. Obligations are always settled before discretionary spending, and the month closes before the first bill of the new month — both are behavioural claims baked into the loop, and neither is cited.

A model day

  1. Income sources run first, so a household paid today can spend today. model.py:843
  2. Consumers run in shuffled order, so nobody is permanently first in the queue. model.py:844
  3. Daily KPIs and grouped mean balances are snapshotted, then the DataCollector reads them. model.py:847-854
  4. The calendar advances one day. model.py:857

A household's day

  1. Month-close — on day 1 only, sweep last month's residual into savings or pension, then zero the accumulators. Runs before bills so a day-1 bill like rent counts toward the new month. model.py:405-406
  2. Settle overdue bills — retry anything carried over, paying the bill plus the late fee if it is now affordable. model.py:409
  3. Pay today's due bills — whichever bills fall on this day-of-month. model.py:410
  4. Service the debt — on day 25 only. model.py:411
  5. Maybe buy something — a single discretionary purchase, at most one per day. model.py:412

The affordability gate

Every outflow passes one test, model.py:322-333. It reserves room for the overdraft fee before allowing the payment, so the floor holds including fees:

projected = self.account.balance - amount
if self.account.balance >= 0 and projected < 0:
    projected -= numbers.OVERDRAFT_FEE_EUR   # reserve room for the fee
return projected >= self._overdraft_floor

The floor is 0.0 for everyone except chronic debtors, who may go one month's debt service negative. So only chronics can ever pay an overdraft fee — for everyone else the balance simply cannot cross zero.

Money is conserved system-wide. Every movement is a paired debit and credit — including the savings sweep (internal, same owner) and the credit draw (lender → household). The sum of all balances equals the sum of all opening balances, tested in tests/test_conservation.py. Interest is the one exception, and it conserves precisely because it writes no transaction: it moves a number, not money.
An accounting asymmetry worth knowing about. The month-end residual is income − bills − debt − discretionary (model.py:426). Overdraft fees are not subtracted, and credit draws are not added to income. So a household that paid fees all month computes a residual slightly larger than what actually happened. The min(residual, balance) cap is what stops this over-sweeping in practice — it is a guard, not a correction.

What this section is not saying. A modelling choice is not a flaw; every ABM is mostly choices. The claim is narrower: a choice that is presented as calibration is a problem, and a choice that drives a headline number while sitting outside the sweep is a problem. Those are the two marked must-fix. The rest simply need to be visible.
