Below is the useful part of the **Banca d’Italia SHIW 2022 paper** for filling the gaps in your ABM population generator. The key point: this paper cannot give you exact individual microdata, but it gives strong **calibration constraints** for household income, wealth, savings, bank deposits, debt burden, and vulnerability.

## 1. What this paper is useful for in your generator

Treat this paper as a **household-level calibration source**, not a transaction-level source.

It is good for:

| ABM variable                | Can use from this paper? | Use as                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | -----------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Household net annual income |                   Partly | Distribution constraints and source-based income multipliers            |
| Income source               |                      Yes | Payroll, self-employed, pension, transfers/not working, property income |
| Equivalized income          |                      Yes | Welfare/income-per-household-member measure                             |
| Wealth                      |                   Partly | Mean, median, top-decile concentration                                  |
| Financial assets            |           Yes, aggregate | Bank/deposit balance proxy                                              |
| Savings behavior            |           Yes, aggregate | Probability of no saving by income quintile                             |
| Debt status and burden      |                      Yes | Debt probabilities by income quartile                                   |
| Liquidity vulnerability     |                      Yes | Low-buffer household class                                              |
| Household composition       |             Not directly | But gives equivalence-scale formula                                     |

The report is based on more than 9,500 interviewed households and reports household income net of income taxes and social security contributions. It also notes that the survey design was changed to better capture high-income households, which matters for your generator because you should explicitly model the upper tail instead of assuming a simple symmetric distribution.

## A. Full income distribution of Italy

**The paper does not give the full income distribution**, but it gives enough constraints to build a realistic synthetic distribution.

Use these facts:

| Income distribution feature           | Value / rule from paper |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------: |
| Mean household income, real net terms | +1.4% from 2020 to 2022 |
| Mean equivalized income               | +1.8% from 2020 to 2022 |
| Mean household income vs 2006         |   still about 10% lower |
| Mean equivalized income vs 2006       |    still about 5% lower |
| Gini index of equivalized income      |                   33.6% |
| Bottom income decile share            |    2.4% of total income |
| Top income decile share               |   27.9% of total income |

The most useful calibration target is the **Gini = 33.6%** and the decile shares: bottom 10% get **2.4%** of total income, top 10% get **27.9%**. 

For your generator, I would not try to directly recreate every euro value from this short report. Instead:

```text
Generate equivalized household income from a fitted distribution.
Constrain it so that:
- Gini ≈ 0.336
- bottom decile receives ≈ 2.4% of income
- top decile receives ≈ 27.9% of income
```

Then convert equivalized income into total household income using household composition:

```text
household_income = equivalized_income × equivalence_scale
```

This is important because your ABM should generate **households first**, then persons inside households, not just isolated individuals.

```text
base_income = draw_from_income_distribution(decile)
```

## C. Household composition

The paper does **not** give the actual distribution of Italian household types, such as single adults, couples, families with children, elderly couples, and so on.

But it gives a very useful rule: the paper uses the **OECD-modified equivalence scale**:

| Household member                | Weight |
| ------------------------------- | -----: |
| Household head                  |    1.0 |
| Each additional member aged 14+ |    0.5 |
| Each child under 14             |    0.3 |

Equivalized income is total household income divided by the number of equivalized adults. 

Use this in your ABM like this:

```python
def equivalence_scale(n_adults: int, n_children_under_14: int) -> float:
    if n_adults < 1:
        raise ValueError("Household must have at least one adult.")
    return 1.0 + 0.5 * (n_adults - 1) + 0.3 * n_children_under_14


def household_income_from_equivalized(
    equivalized_income: float,
    n_adults: int,
    n_children_under_14: int,
) -> float:
    return equivalized_income * equivalence_scale(n_adults, n_children_under_14)
```

Example:

```text
Single adult:
scale = 1.0

Couple without children:
scale = 1.5

Couple with two children under 14:
scale = 1.0 + 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.3 = 2.1
```

So if two households have the same equivalized income, the larger household receives a larger total household income.

## E. Starting wallet cash distribution

The paper does **not** report wallet cash.

Do not pretend it does.

What you can do is derive a behavioral proxy:

```text
wallet_cash = short-term transaction buffer
```

Use your payment-method paper for cash-use probabilities, and use this SHIW paper only to condition liquidity:

| Household type             | Wallet-cash logic                                                                    |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Low income, liquidity poor | low bank balance, possibly higher cash reliance, but small absolute cash buffer      |
| High income                | lower relative need for cash, but can hold more absolute cash                        |
| Pensioner household        | potentially more cash-oriented, but this must come from your payment paper, not SHIW |
| Self-employed              | possibly higher cash variability, but again needs payment-source evidence            |

A simple generator rule:

```python
wallet_cash = min(
    monthly_income * cash_buffer_rate,
    bank_balance * liquidity_access_rate,
)
```

Where `cash_buffer_rate` should come from your payment-method data, not this SHIW report.

Use SHIW only to say:

```text
Low-income households are more financially constrained.
More than 70% of households in the lowest income quintile did not save.
```

That supports low cash buffers for the bottom quintile.

Net wealth means:


The paper also warns that wealth estimates may be distorted because respondents can underreport or have difficulty valuing assets. 

For your generator:

```text
Use mean = €296,000 and median = €152,000 as calibration targets.
```

## G. Exact daily purchase frequency per person

The paper cannot answer this.

It only gives broad consumption changes:

| Group                           | 2022 spending change |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------: |
| Mean household spending         |           +5.7% real |
| Top income quintile spending    |                 +11% |
| Bottom income quintile spending |                  −2% |

The paper says the top quintile recovered strongly in non-essential goods and services, while the bottom quintile continued cutting spending and sometimes delayed bills or relied on family, friends, or public grants. 

Use this as a **budget constraint**, not as daily frequency.

Recommended approach:

```text
1. Use another source for daily/weekly purchase frequency.
2. Use SHIW to scale monthly spending budgets by income quintile.
3. Generate transactions until the household reaches its monthly consumption budget.
```

Behavioral rule:

```text
Bottom quintile:
    fewer discretionary purchases
    higher probability of delayed utility bills
    higher probability of receiving family/public support

Top quintile:
    higher discretionary spending
    higher durable/non-essential purchase probability
```

```text

```

Financially vulnerable households are defined as households with equivalized income below the median and annual debt-service ratio above 30% of monetary income. They are **1.5% of all households** and **7% of indebted households**. 

Liquidity-poor households are those whose liquid financial assets are too low to avoid poverty for three months. Among indebted households, **37.4%** were liquidity poor. 

Use these as ABM classes:

```text
normal_household
low_savings_household
indebted_household
financially_vulnerable_household
liquidity_poor_household
```

# 4. Recommended household generator structure

Use this order:

```text
1. Generate household composition from your demographic source.
2. Compute equivalence scale using OECD-modified scale.
3. Assign region, age, education, gender from your other source.
4. Assign primary income source:
       payroll employee
       self-employed
       pension
       transfer/not working
5. Draw equivalized income from calibrated distribution.
6. Convert to household income.
7. Assign income decile/quintile.
8. Assign wealth decile correlated with income decile.
9. Generate net wealth using mean/median/top-decile constraints.
10. Generate financial assets.
11. Generate deposit/bank balance from financial assets × deposit share.
12. Generate debt status and debt service.
13. Generate savings status.
14. Generate monthly spending budget.
15. Generate transactions using your payment-method paper.
```

Minimal schema:

```python
@dataclass
class Household:
    household_id: int
    region: str

    n_adults: int
    n_children_under_14: int
    equivalence_scale: float

    primary_income_source: str
    annual_net_income: float
    equivalized_income: float
    income_decile: int
    income_quintile: int

    net_wealth: float
    financial_assets: float
    deposit_balance: float
    wallet_cash: float

    has_debt: bool
    debt_type: str | None
    annual_debt_service: float

    saves_this_year: bool
    liquidity_poor: bool
    financially_vulnerable: bool
```

# 5. Clean interpretation for your thesis

You can write something like this:

> The SHIW 2022 report is used to calibrate the household layer of the agent-based model. It provides aggregate constraints for net household income, income inequality, source-of-income differences, equivalized income, financial assets, net wealth, savings behavior, debt burden, and financial vulnerability. The model therefore treats the household, not the individual, as the primary economic unit. Individual agents inherit household-level financial constraints while retaining their own demographic and payment-behavior attributes from other data sources.

And:

> The SHIW report does not provide exact daily transaction frequencies, wallet cash holdings, or a complete household-composition distribution. These variables are therefore generated from other sources or modeled as assumptions. However, SHIW provides the macro-financial constraints that keep these assumptions realistic, especially across income quintiles, wealth deciles, income sources, and vulnerability classes.

In practical terms: use your payment paper for **how people pay**, and use this SHIW paper for **how much financial capacity their household has**.
