Below is the paper distilled into an ABM-ready specification. Key caution: this is **not a full consumer-spending generator**. It is specifically a **wire-transfer generator**: rents, salaries, pensions, taxes, bills, service fees, invoices, settlements, and family/community expense-sharing. The authors explicitly say wire transfers are impractical for fast purchases and are used more for fixed or large payments such as rents and salaries; cards/cash are excluded, so shopping/retail behaviour is under-observed. ([Springer][1]) ([Springer][1]) 

## 1. Core behavioral picture

The paper’s model of Italian wire-transfer behaviour is:

> Money generally flows from high-degree legal entities — firms, finance, institutions, large employers — toward lower-degree natural persons, then is partially redistributed inside households, families, and small local communities through rent, housing costs, shared expenses, and transfers among relatives.

This is not purely hierarchical: the whole network is disassortative, but natural-person subcommunities are locally assortative and clustered. High-degree hubs pay many low-degree agents; low-degree agents pay firms/institutions and also exchange money among each other. The authors describe this as a strong hierarchical pattern mixed with small, tightly clustered natural-person communities. ([Springer][1])

## 2. Agent types and attributes to implement

| ABM entity      |                                     Paper meaning | Attributes available / implied                                                      | Behavioural role                                                                                                          |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `NaturalPerson` |                    Individual human account owner | gender, birth year/age, region inferred from branch, account ownership structure    | Low-degree nodes; receive salaries/pensions; pay rent, mortgage, bills, taxes, fees; share expenses with family/community |
| `LegalEntity`   | Company, institution, public/private organisation | NACE/ATECO sector, region, legal status, account degree/strength                    | Medium/high-degree nodes; employers, utilities, finance institutions, businesses, public administration, suppliers        |
| `Account`       |                                        Graph node | one or multiple owners; natural/legal/no-info/mislabelled/automatic-operation label | Transaction endpoint                                                                                                      |
| `Transfer`      |                          Directed multigraph edge | payer, payee, timestamp, amount, channel, purpose text                              | Monetary event                                                                                                            |

Dataset scale: more than **12M account owners**: 11.5M natural persons and 500K legal entities, over **12.7M accounts**. The transfer dataset has **100M transactions** worth more than **€1000B**, Jan 2016–Dec 2017. ([Springer][1])

Account ownership distribution:

| Owners per account |                          Share |
| ------------------ | -----------------------------: |
| Single owner       |                          69.0% |
| Two owners         |                          23.9% |
| More than two      |                           7.1% |
| More than 20       | rare; mainly business accounts |

Natural persons own **88%** of accounts, legal entities **12%**. But legal entities generate **more than twice** as many wire transfers as natural persons and transfer an order of magnitude more total money. The largest amount is legal-to-legal, while the largest number of transfers is legal-to-natural. ([Springer][1])

## 3. Payment categories you should code

Use four directed transaction regimes.

| Flow                            | Purpose inferred from payment text                        | Network signature                                                         | ABM rule                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `NaturalPerson → NaturalPerson` | rent, housing expenses, family/community expense sharing  | low-degree to low-degree; locally assortative; high clustering            | Recurring monthly or semi-regular transfers inside household/family/local cluster          |
| `LegalEntity → NaturalPerson`   | salary, pension                                           | high-degree/legal to low-degree/natural; employer-employee band           | Monthly salary/pension payments; end-of-month spike; extra June/December payments possible |
| `NaturalPerson → LegalEntity`   | payment, fee, settlement; also taxes, bills, service fees | low-degree natural persons pay businesses/institutions of variable degree | Regular bills/taxes/fees; monthly or scheduled                                             |
| `LegalEntity → LegalEntity`     | settlement, invoice                                       | noisy, broad-spectrum B2B; 18% of transfers but 42% of money              | Invoice/supply-chain settlement; large amounts; sector-dependent                           |

The paper’s text analysis explicitly finds those four purpose groups from January 2016 payment-purpose text. ([Springer][1])

For a generator, treat wire transfers as **relationship-driven payments**, not impulse purchases. More than **82%** of transfers are repeated more than once between the same pair of nodes, consistent with contracts such as buyer–seller or worker–employer relationships. ([Springer][1])

## 4. Network topology and degree distributions

Represent the system as a **directed multigraph**:

```text
node = bank account
edge = wire transfer from payer account to payee account
edge attributes = timestamp, amount, channel, purpose
node attributes = natural/legal/no-info, region, age/gender if natural, NACE sector if legal
```

The authors use exactly this graph formulation and emphasize that many payments, such as rents and salaries, repeat monthly between the same account pair. ([Springer][1])

### Degree distribution

The degree distribution is heavy-tailed / power-law-like:

| Distribution | Exponent |
| ------------ | -------: |
| Total degree | γ = 2.24 |
| In-degree    | γ = 2.27 |
| Out-degree   | γ = 2.23 |

The fit thresholds reported in Fig. 5 are: `xmin_total = 111`, `xmin_in = 93`, `xmin_out = 29`. ([Springer][1])

Natural persons concentrate at low degrees; no natural person has degree above **2500** in the 2017 network. Legal-entity accounts can exceed **10⁶** connections. Natural-person degree distribution has a visible spike around **degree ≈ 12**, which the authors interpret as a sign of monthly payments. ([Springer][1])

### Connectivity

The giant connected component does **not** emerge like an Erdős–Rényi phase transition. Instead, the network grows around a stable core of the top **1% highest-degree nodes**. In 2017, **94%** of nodes are in the GCC; **94.5%** of highest-degree first-percentile nodes are already in the GCC in January 2017. ([Springer][1])

For ABM initialization: create hubs first, connect them early, then attach low-degree natural persons and smaller businesses around them. Do not generate the network by uniform random attachment.

## 5. Assortativity, clustering, and local communities

The global network is strongly **disassortative**: low-degree nodes tend to connect to high-degree nodes, and high-degree nodes tend to connect to low-degree nodes. Degree is also a rough proxy for type: low-degree nodes are mostly natural persons; high-degree nodes are mostly legal entities. ([Springer][1])

However, the low-degree natural-person subnetwork behaves differently:

| Pattern                                                        | Meaning for ABM                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Low-degree natural persons are clustered                       | Build households/families/local communities with triangle closure                    |
| Natural-person ↔ natural-person transfers are more assortative | Similar-degree people exchange money among themselves                                |
| High-degree legal entities have low clustering                 | Employers/institutions pay many disconnected recipients                              |
| Medium businesses sit between both worlds                      | They operate locally, so they may pay/be paid by people who also know/pay each other |

The paper states that natural persons pay each other for personal reasons inside local communities and families — housing expenses, mortgage payments, etc. — while big companies spread payments across many distant receivers. ([Springer][1])

## 6. Degree bands you can use

The paper identifies degree band `[10, 100]` as the main natural-person activity regime. The reasoning: a regular account owner may receive salary/pension monthly, pay rent/mortgage monthly, pay bills monthly, and perform additional expenditures. In heatmaps, this range shows dense activity and natural persons dominate this degree range. ([Springer][1])

ABM practical classes:

| Degree class     | Likely type                                                        | Behaviour                                                |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| `k < 10`         | inactive/light natural persons, small entities                     | few transfers/year                                       |
| `10 ≤ k ≤ 100`   | ordinary active natural persons                                    | salary/pension in; rent/bills/taxes/family transfers out |
| `100 < k < 2500` | active natural persons, small firms, freelancers, local businesses | mixed behaviour; possible mislabelling                   |
| `k ≥ 2500`       | legal entities/hubs                                                | employers, finance, institutions, high-volume businesses |
| `k > 10⁶`        | extreme legal hubs                                                 | systemic payment hubs                                    |

The paper warns that some high-degree “natural persons” and low-degree “legal entities” may be mislabelled or accounts repurposed by freelancers/business owners. Treat labels probabilistically near the boundary. ([Springer][1])

## 7. Direction of money flow

The directional hierarchy is measurable:

| Direction relative to degree             | Share of transfers |
| ---------------------------------------- | -----------------: |
| Higher-degree payer → lower-degree payee |              68.9% |
| Lower-degree payer → higher-degree payee |              29.4% |

Legal-to-legal transfers are only about **18%** of wire transfers but account for about **42%** of circulating money. This matters: B2B flows are fewer but much larger. ([Springer][1])

ABM implication: generate **frequency** mostly from salary/bill/rent relationships, but generate **amount mass** from legal-entity and B2B flows.

## 8. Bow-tie structure

The paper’s bow-tie decomposition:

| Component      | Share of nodes | Interpretation                                                   |
| -------------- | -------------: | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SCC            |         24.03% | Economic core; strongly connected; main circulating money engine |
| IN             |         18.33% | Upstream accounts feeding SCC                                    |
| OUT            |         40.84% | Downstream sink; money exits wire-transfer network here          |
| Tubes/Tendrils |          9.11% | Peripheral directed paths                                        |
| Disconnected   |          7.68% | Isolated components                                              |

OUT is the largest component and mostly natural persons. Money reaches OUT and is not re-injected via wire transfer, possibly because it is then spent by card/cash. SCC is only 24% of nodes but produces almost 80% of transfers and more than 85% of transferred amount. ([Springer][1])

## 9. Temporal behaviour

Calendar effects are strong:

| Time pattern                                   | Interpretation                                      |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Few transfers on weekends and Italian holidays | Banking/business-day effect                         |
| End-of-month increase in number and amount     | Salaries                                            |
| June and December peaks                        | Bonus salaries and taxes                            |
| Christmas most active                          | High economic activity                              |
| August most depressed                          | Vacations, company closures, card/cash substitution |
| Number of transfers more regular than amount   | Large legal-entity payments drive amount volatility |

The paper notes that daily transaction counts are more stable, while total amount fluctuates heavily and is harder to explain without NLP on purpose text. ([Springer][1])

ABM scheduling:

```text
For each simulated day:
    if weekend/holiday: suppress most wire transfers
    if last business days of month: boost salary/pension/rent/bill flows
    if June or December end-month: boost salary bonus/tax flows
    if August: reduce firm/legal-entity activity strongly
    if Christmas period: increase activity
```

## 10. Regional behaviour in Italy

Regional wire-transfer totals are strongly aligned with regional GDP. The paper reports outgoing wire-transfer total amount vs regional GDP correlation of **r = 0.97**, **p = 4.1 × 10⁻¹³**. All mutual correlations among regional GDP, incoming/outgoing transfer number, and incoming/outgoing amount are high, though actual transfer values are normalized because of bank policy. ([Springer][1])

North–South inequality is large:

| Regional statistic                                                                   | Paper value |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------: |
| South Italy GDP per capita vs Centre-North                                           |   45% lower |
| Wire-transfer amount received by natural persons in Southern regions vs Centre-North | 44.6% lower |

Northern accounts are richer on average. Lombardy and Lazio emerge as the main economic centres. The “richest club” is Lombardy, Piedmont, Veneto, Emilia-Romagna, and Lazio; Campania is the only southern region with a non-negligible exchange with that club. ([Springer][1])

ABM regional multiplier idea:

```text
region_income_multiplier:
    North, especially Lombardy/Piedmont/Veneto/Emilia-Romagna: high
    Centre, especially Lazio/Rome: high for professional services
    South/Islands: lower income and transfer volume
    Campania: strongest Southern connector, receives more than it sends
```

## 12. ABM behaviour rules

A compact implementation design:

```python
class AgentType:
    NATURAL = "natural_person"
    LEGAL = "legal_entity"

class TransferPurpose:
    SALARY = "salary"
    PENSION = "pension"
    RENT_HOUSING = "rent_housing"
    FAMILY_EXPENSE_SHARE = "family_expense_share"
    BILL_TAX_FEE = "bill_tax_fee"
    B2B_INVOICE = "b2b_invoice"
    B2B_SETTLEMENT = "b2b_settlement"
    PUBLIC_ADMIN = "public_admin"
    OTHER = "other"
```

Suggested generation order:

1. Generate regions with GDP/income multipliers: North high, Lazio high for professional services, South lower.
2. Generate accounts: mostly natural persons; smaller but highly active legal entities.
3. Assign natural-person accounts into households/family/local clusters.
4. Assign legal entities into NACE sectors and bow-tie components, with large hubs concentrated in SCC.
5. Sample degree targets from power-law-like distributions: γ around 2.2.
6. Create core SCC first, especially legal hubs.
7. Add recurring relationships:

   * employer → worker monthly
   * pension/finance/public institution → pensioner monthly
   * tenant/person → landlord/person or real-estate/legal monthly
   * person → utilities/tax/service providers monthly/periodic
   * firm → firm invoice/settlement, sector-dependent
   * family/community expense sharing among natural persons
8. Apply temporal calendar modifiers.
9. Allow most money to circulate in SCC, then drain to OUT.
10. Let OUT agents spend much of their money through unobserved channels: cash/card, not wire transfer.

## 14. What the paper does **not** give you

The paper does **not** provide a clean transaction-amount distribution by purpose or household income bracket. It also hides absolute regional wire-transfer values, showing normalized amounts because of bank policy. It excludes cash/card payments and excludes transactions involving other banks’ customers. Therefore, for a full consumer-spending generator, you need an additional module for card/cash retail consumption and external-bank interactions. The paper itself says wire-transfer-only data can understate shopping and distort sectors such as commerce. ([Springer][1])
