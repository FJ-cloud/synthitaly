Section 1

What these tests can and cannot establish

Three papers from the consumer-credit literature are re-run here against this model's own output. Each was chosen because it classifies exactly the kind of consumer this model produces — a debtor or not, a transactor or a revolver, an account that will fall 90 days behind or will not.

The fourth paper, Fagiolo, Moneta & Windrum (2007), is not a method. It is the reason this section exists. Their under-determination problem — “What happens when different models are consistent with the data that is used for empirical validation?” — is the trap a page like this walks into if it reports a high score as a success.

A classifier scoring well here is not evidence that the model is right. The model generated both the behaviour and the labels, so a classifier is only asking whether the generating mechanism left a detectable trace in the ledger. The interesting readings are therefore the places where the answer is wrong in a specific, mechanical way — and there are three of them.

The premise of the transactor/revolver paper is inverted here

So et al. build their whole cascade on the claim that “since transactors pay off all their balance each period, they cannot default and so all Transactors must be Goods.” In this model the Bad rate among transactors is 28.0% against 15.6% among revolvers. Holding debt is a marker of safety here, not of risk.

Distress is a standing condition, not an event

Khandani et al. devote a table to “straight-rollers” — accounts current at the forecast date that go 90 days past due anyway, which they call “a harder learning problem”. In this model that population is essentially empty. Nobody goes from a clean account to written off; arrears are a persistent liquidity state that a consumer is either in or not.

The difficulty is wrong in both directions

Real credit-risk models land in a middle band — Gini around 0.52, kappa 0.66–0.79, AUC 0.83–0.89. This model produces either near-noise or near-certainty depending on which label is asked for, and almost nothing in between. That gap is the substantive result of this page.

Section 2

Which variables are switched on

Every table on this page names the variable setting it came from. There are three, and the difference between them is the whole argument about what the model can and cannot be said to predict.

Set A — Everything the simulator knows

All columns, including those that encode the answer by construction. Useful only as a ceiling: it says what the score looks like when the answer is in the inputs.

Set B — Only what a bank could observe

Columns that reveal the label mechanically are switched off. This is the honest bank's-eye view and the default for every headline number.

Set C — Observable behaviour, arrears switched off

Set B without the account's own arrears counters, which are near-deterministic for the forward label. Asks whether spending and income behaviour carries the signal once the answer cannot be read off an arrears counter.

Set membership is derived from the data, never hand-listed, so it cannot drift out of step with the columns actually built — src/synthitaly/panel.py.

Section 3 · Paper 1

So, Thomas, Seow & Mues — the Transactor/Revolver scorecard

What the paper does. Four logistic scorecards on 6,308 Hong Kong credit-card accounts (1,577 Bad, 4,731 Good — a 25.0% Bad rate). Each is built by weight-of-evidence coarse classification, then stepwise logistic regression, then ten-fold cross validation done by deciles. They report the mean Gini over the ten holdouts and compare Model 1 with Model 4 using the DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson test.

  1. Model 1 — Good/Bad over everybody. Their Gini 0.522.
  2. Model 2 — Transactor/Revolver over everybody.
  3. Model 3 — Good/Bad restricted to Revolvers. Their Gini 0.519.
  4. Model 4 — the composite P(G|x) = P(T|x) + P(R|x)·P(G|x,R). Their Gini 0.522, statistically indistinguishable from Model 1.

What is replicated. All four models and the full procedure — WoE binning, the univariate screen, stepwise selection, ten decile folds and the DeLong test. The scorecard is cross-sectional, as an application scorecard is: characteristics over the trailing window at one origination month, outcome over the following performance period. Bin boundaries, selection and coefficients are all fitted inside the fold, so every Gini below is out of sample.

The paper's structural premise does not hold here. So et al. argue that transactors cannot default. In this model the Bad rate among transactors is 28.0% (n=633) against 15.6% among revolvers (n=167) — the relationship is not weaker, it is reversed. The reason is mechanical: _assign_debt hands debt out with a probability that rises with income quartile, so debtors are drawn from the better-off half of the population, while the consumers who actually fail to pay bills are the unemployed and transfer-income households, who were never given debt in the first place. Nothing in the model makes debt cause distress.

Broken out by debtor subtype the point is starker still. Two of the three debtor archetypes reach 90 days past due exactly zero times across the whole panel, while the consumers carrying no debt at all are the ones who default:

Where So et al. found the composite statistically indistinguishable from the plain scorecard, here routing the Good/Bad decision through the Transactor/Revolver split destroys discrimination — Model 1 averages 0.95 against Model 4's 0.81, and DeLong rejects the equality in 8 of 10 runs where it could be computed at all. That is what happens when a segmentation carries no information about the outcome: Model 2's Gini averages just 0.36, the same weak signal the existing Study B reports as AUC 0.697 for is_debtor.

The two Transactor/Revolver definitions

Both were run: behavioural (carried a debt balance across a month boundary, which is the paper's own definition) and assigned (the is_debtor attribute the model drew). The gap between them is exactly zero until month 17, because no climber has finished repaying before then — until that point the two definitions are the same variable. After it they separate as climbers clear their principal and convert from Revolver to Transactor, which the static attribute cannot express.

Section 4 · Paper 2

Khandani, Kim & Lo (2010) — CART forecasts of 90+ day delinquency

What the paper does. Generalized classification and regression trees (CART, Breiman et al. 1984) on a commercial bank's customers, forecasting 90-days-or-more delinquency over a 3-, 6- or 12-month forward window. Transaction inputs are averaged over the prior six months. The evaluation walks forward: their Table 5 lists ten calibration/testing periods in which the model is trained only on delinquencies observable at the forecast date, “to minimize the effects of look-ahead bias”. Their delinquency rate runs 2.0%–2.5%; their kappa 0.66–0.79, AUC 0.83-0.89 across their ten evaluation windows.

What is replicated. CART — scikit-learn implements the same Breiman algorithm the paper cites, so this is the method rather than a substitute — the six-month trailing averages, the forward-walking calibration, all three horizons, and the full evaluation block: the average-forecast separation of their Table 7, the straight-roller restriction of their Table 8, the ROC tangency threshold of their Figure 16, and the kappa of their Table 9 read against Landis & Koch. Their §3.1 balance-to-income ratio and §3.2 income-shock variable are carried as model inputs.

The straight-rollers — their Table 8

Khandani et al. single out accounts that are current at the forecast date and go 90 days past due anyway, calling it “a harder learning problem” and reporting that their model still separates the two groups. In this model that population does not exist. Across every evaluable period at every horizon, the number of clean accounts that reached 90 days past due is zero for the write-off outcome. Arrears here are a standing liquidity condition: a bill must already have gone unpaid for most of the write-off horizon before it can cross it, and consumers who can pay simply keep paying. The model generates no default shock.

Section 5 · Paper 3

Butaru, Chen, Clark, Das, Lo & Siddique (2015) — the three-model horse race

What the paper does. Three classifiers on the same 87 attributes, run against one another across 6 anonymous banks and 5.7-6.6 million credit-card accounts: a C4.5 decision tree (Weka's J48), a ridge logistic regression, and a random forest of 20 trees. The target is an account 90 or more days past due, forecast two, three and four quarters out. Models are re-estimated on rolling windows and never see future data. Their finding: C4.5 and random-forest models outperform logistic regression, with accuracy from 63.8% at the worst bank to 81.6% at the best.

What differs, stated plainly

The tree is a partial substitution

C4.5 is not in scikit-learn. DecisionTreeClassifier(criterion="entropy") uses C4.5's splitting rule, so that half matches; C4.5's error-based pruning and multi-way splits have no equivalent, and min_samples_leaf stands in for them. The ridge logistic is an exact counterpart of the objective they write out, and the forest keeps their 20 trees rather than being ‘improved’.

Six seeds, not six banks

Their six banks are six genuinely different populations, which is what makes their spread meaningful. This model has one population, so the six portfolios here are six seeds and the spread between them is Monte-Carlo error and nothing else. That is a materially weaker claim and is labelled as such in the table.

A longer simulation

This section runs 1440 simulated days rather than the repo's 720. At 720 the three- and four-quarter horizons have no evaluable origination month, because a forecast needs a trailing window, a full horizon ahead, and training labels that have already closed. Extending the simulation is the honest fix; truncating the design would not be a replication.

The two sit at opposite ends of the same axis and neither is in the middle where real credit models live. is_debtor is drawn from a random tilt on a single hidden flag, so conditional on vulnerability it is close to noise and 0.70 is a ceiling, not a tuning failure. The delinquency label is the opposite: it follows almost deterministically from income source — unemployed households write bills off at 10× the rate of payroll ones — and the ledger reveals income source almost perfectly through the salary credits.

What this points at. The model has no mechanism connecting debt to distress. Debt is assigned by income quartile and then serviced by a rule that depends only on the subtype; delinquency is driven by whether the current account happens to be empty when a bill falls due. The two systems run alongside each other and never interact, which is exactly why the transactor bad rate (28.0%) exceeds the revolver bad rate (15.6%). A debt-service burden that actually competed with bill payments for the same euros would be the smallest change that makes all three papers' premises hold at once.
Section 7

Problems, stated plainly

Weight of evidence inflates its own significance

WoE is fitted against the label, so a WoE-transformed column carries in-sample signal even when the raw variable is pure noise. Measured directly: the likelihood-ratio stepwise test admits a noise column 7% of the time on raw inputs — about its nominal rate — and 99% of the time on WoE-transformed noise. The guard is a univariate screen on out-of-sample information value, which the paper also describes. An in-sample screen does not work: noise scores IV ≈ 0.03 at n=1,500 and ≈ 0.006 at n=8,000, so no fixed threshold is right for both.

The characteristic list has seen all the rows

Following the paper, the shortlist of characteristics is chosen once rather than inside each fold. Every bin boundary, coefficient and reported Gini still comes from the nine training deciles alone, but the list is mildly optimistic. This is inherited from the paper's own procedure, not a shortcut taken here.

Class imbalance at 90+ DPD

Around 3% of consumer-months, close to the papers' own 2.0–2.5%.
